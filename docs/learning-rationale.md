# Learning rationale

Research notes: 21–22 October 2026. Lantern Garden is a foundational on-ramp for a beginner of any age, not a complete school curriculum or Python course. The original three-activity opening is Lesson 1 of the twenty-lesson course.

The opening aims to help a learner predict a short sequence, connect each executed line to position and facing, repair a wrong turn, and create a route on a different map. These are intended outcomes, not measured learning results. The proposed first visit is 10–15 minutes; later 15–25-minute visits are also design estimates, not deadlines or evidence-based optimal durations.

The summaries below distinguish published evidence or curriculum guidance from this project's design judgments. The cited studies did not evaluate Lantern Garden.

## Seven evidence-informed design decisions

1. **Read before writing; gradually hand over ownership.** Published evidence: Sentance, Waite and Kallia evaluated PRIMM with 493 students aged 11–14 in 13 schools over 8–12 weeks, reporting better post-test performance than a control group. The framework moves through prediction, running, investigation, modification and making. Design judgment: compress that structure into a supplied route, a one-command repair, and a fresh short route. A solo 15-minute game lacks the study’s duration and classroom dialogue, so its results cannot be claimed for this chapter. [Original PRIMM study and abstract](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/229013/); [official NCCE explanation of the stages](https://media.teachcomputing.org/QR_11_PRIMM_a57d70eb85.pdf).

2. **Keep the first example complete, then change only one demand.** Published evidence: Margulieux, Guzdial and Catrambone’s programming experiment found benefits from instructional materials that labeled the underlying subgoals when novices learned App Inventor. Design judgment: label the activity purpose (“Read the route”, “Find the turn”, “Plan a new route”), preserve a working example, and introduce one wrong turn before open composition. These labels and this exact command set were not tested in that study. [Original 2012 paper, university repository PDF](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/bitstreams/ea5acfed-1bc8-4169-bc16-b2f990f31f2a/download).

3. **Let the learner see and control each step.** Published evidence: Mayer and Chandler’s two narrated-animation experiments found transfer benefits, but not retention benefits, from learner-controlled pacing under their tested conditions. Design judgment: offer Step as well as Run, slow visible movement, repeatable runs, a current-line marker, and plain-language state (“Facing north”). Connect a line directly with its map effect. The study was about lightning explanations, not code tracing; it motivates this pacing choice rather than validating this interface. [Original study, full text](https://tecfa.unige.ch/tecfa/teaching/methodo/Mayer_Chandler01.pdf).

4. **Teach debugging as comparing a plan with an observation.** Curriculum basis, not an efficacy trial: the K–12 Computer Science Framework describes program development as iterative design, implementation, review and fixing unexpected behavior. Design judgment: ask what should happen, show what did happen, identify the first divergence, change one command, then rerun from the same starting state. Give specific descriptive feedback, not “Wrong!” No lives, score deductions, timer, or restart punishment. The non-punitive treatment is a learner-experience choice, not a causal claim established by this curriculum source. [Official framework, algorithms and program development](https://k12cs.org/framework-statements-by-concept/).

5. **Ask for small acts of recall, not just more watching.** Published evidence: Roediger and Karpicke’s two prose-learning experiments found advantages for prior retrieval on delayed tests, although repeated study performed better at the immediate five-minute test. Design judgment: after a supported run, ask “Does turning move Pip to a new tile?” and later “Which command would change Pip’s facing without moving?” Make answers low-stakes and explain immediately. A first guess before learning is prediction, not evidence of retrieval practice; the recap is the closer retrieval application. Programming benefits remain to be tested. [Original study, APS publisher abstract](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x/).

6. **Revisit the same idea later, without pretending one session is spaced learning.** Published evidence: Cepeda and colleagues studied more than 1,350 people learning facts, varying review gaps and final-test delays; useful spacing depended on the intended retention interval. Design judgment: the opening recap revisits sequencing after an intervening activity. A proposed revisit tomorrow and another next week are practical scheduling suggestions, not an implemented reminder or an experimentally optimal programming schedule. Repeating within 15 minutes is not equivalent to the study’s longer-term spacing. No reminder is scheduled or promised. [Original 2008 study, publisher page](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x).

7. **Finish with a new route using the same ideas, not an unrelated new concept.** Curriculum basis: PRIMM’s Make stage asks learners to use familiar structures to solve a new problem. Programming evidence from subgoal-labeled examples also makes transfer worth examining. Design judgment: change the start/goal geometry but keep step(), left() and right(), with no loops, variables or conditionals yet. Count an independently successful route as evidence of this narrow task, not proof of general transfer; invite a brief explanation of why its turn works. [NCCE PRIMM guidance](https://media.teachcomputing.org/QR_11_PRIMM_a57d70eb85.pdf); [original programming experiment](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/bitstreams/ea5acfed-1bc8-4169-bc16-b2f990f31f2a/download).

## The five-act progression

The [curriculum](curriculum.md) specifies the implemented twenty-lesson order, including 41 available activities and 40 required. Lesson 19 offers either of two projects. Lesson 1 keeps its original read/repair/make sequence; later lessons often use two activities and a compact explanatory check. That exact order and rhythm are design judgments, not research-established optimal pacing.

Familiar ideas recur in later care rounds: facing in opposite-direction rows; sequence order in repeat bodies and shared routines; conditionals in helpers and contrasting worlds; debugging in syntax, shared jobs, and termination. These are opportunities for retrieval and near transfer. Only actual visits separated over time could establish spaced practice. Adapt or split visits to the learner.

Support fades when it serves the concept. Supplied programs, specific repairs, visible routine bodies, and contrasting states precede more independent composition. The current authored copy in `src/curriculum/` is the implementation reference; a research-inspired example is not evidence that a learner understands it.

## What still needs learner evidence

Observe actual duration, clarity, accessibility, story appeal, clue use, frustration/recovery, explanations, retention, and transfer to new tasks. Preserve work and allow retries without penalties. Use descriptive feedback and ask why a change works, rather than treating a completed route or a two-choice retry as a mastery grade.

The stored zero-clue flag is not proof of unaided authorship, especially for supplied programs. Alternate correct plans may use inline actions rather than the intended abstraction. The authored final variation is near transfer, not randomized or delayed assessment. Automated engine variations test execution, not human learning.

The garden language is not Python. Its optional final note points toward a later explicit bridge for variables, parameters, return values, collections, nested loops, and classes. No automated test establishes learning, long-term retention, general-purpose programming proficiency, or readiness for that bridge.
